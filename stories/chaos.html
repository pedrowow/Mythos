In the beginning  ·  The Creation Cycle, Part I of VI

Chaos and the First Void

Before the gods named the world, before light cleaved from darkness, there was only Chaos — boundless, purposeless, and absolute.

Michelangelo, Creation of Adam, c. 1512 — Sistine Chapel, Vatican

The Greeks did not begin their creation story with a god who made something from nothing. They began with a condition. Chaos — from the ancient Greek khaos, meaning a yawning gap, an open chasm — was not a being with intentions. It was the raw state of everything before form existed: darkness folded into darkness, matter without boundary, time without measure.

This is one of the most important distinctions in Greek mythology compared to other ancient traditions. There is no craftsman god, no divine plan, no six days of labour. The universe does not begin with an act of will. It begins with a condition becoming unstable — and then things simply emerging from it.

From Chaos came the first entities, though "entities" is almost too solid a word for what they were. Nyx, the Night, and Erebus, the deep Darkness — not children, exactly, but separations. Parts of Chaos becoming distinct enough to have names. And from Nyx and Erebus came Aether, the bright upper air, and Hemera, the Day: the first true opposites, born from the union of two darknesses.

Side myth Nyx's terrible children

Hesiod does not let Nyx rest with just Aether and Hemera. Alone, without any partner, she goes on to bear a whole brood of dark personifications: Moros (Doom), Ker (violent death), Thanatos (Death) and his twin Hypnos (Sleep), the Oneiroi (Dreams), Nemesis (Retribution), Apate (Deceit), Geras (Old Age), and Eris (Strife).

None of these are villains exactly; they are conditions, the same way Chaos itself is a condition rather than a character. Night gives birth to everything that erodes or ends a life, simply because darkness and endings belong together conceptually. It is one of the clearest examples of Greek myth using genealogy as a kind of philosophical shorthand.

"In truth, at first Chaos came to be, but next wide-bosomed Earth." — Hesiod, Theogony

Gaia rises

Then came Gaia. Not summoned, not created. She arose from Chaos as the earth arises from deep water — as if she had always been there, waiting for the right conditions to surface. She was the ground itself, the foundation of everything physical that would follow.

Anselm Feuerbach, Gaea, 1875 — Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen, Munich

Gaia's emergence is described by Hesiod in the Theogony as one of the most fundamental acts of the cosmos. She is not a goddess in the Olympian sense — not a figure who walks and schemes and falls in love. She is the world's own body, conscious and ancient. Her relationships with the forces she generates are closer to geology than romance: she produces Uranus, the Sky, from herself alone, without a partner, so that he might cover her entirely and provide a home for the gods who would come.

This is worth pausing on. Gaia's first child is her own future husband. She creates the being who will dominate her. Greek mythology has always been comfortable with the unsettling, the paradoxical, and the intimate made cosmic.

Eros and the principle of connection

Alongside Gaia and Nyx, Hesiod places Eros — but not the cherubic figure of later myth, the mischievous child of Aphrodite with his bow and arrows. This primordial Eros is something older and stranger: the force of attraction itself, the principle that makes things come together rather than drift apart.

His presence at the very beginning of creation is the Greeks saying something profound about how the universe works. Without a force pulling matter toward matter, there is no structure. Without desire, there is no cosmos. Chaos is the condition of everything separated; Eros is what makes it cohere. The universe is built, at its root, on the logic of things wanting to be near each other.

Also from Chaos came Tartarus — the abyss beneath the earth, as far below the surface as the sky is above it. He would later become the prison of the defeated Titans, but in the beginning he was simply depth itself, the counterpart to Uranus's height.

Side myth A different Eros: the Orphic egg

Hesiod's account is not the only creation story the Greeks told. The Orphic tradition, a mystery-cult cosmology attested from later sources, has Eros hatch fully formed from a great silver egg laid by Chronos (Time, not to be confused with the Titan Cronus) and Ananke (Necessity). In this version Eros is often called Phanes, "the shining one," and it is his birth, not a condition arising unbidden, that starts creation moving.

The two accounts were never reconciled and were not meant to be: Hesiod's Theogony was public, civic, recited at festivals; the Orphic cosmology belonged to initiates and mystery rites, where cosmology and initiation were treated as the same act.

The shape of the world before the world

Thomas Cole, The Savage State from The Course of Empire, 1834 — a vision of a world before order

What Hesiod is describing, before anything else, is a cosmology with four axes: Chaos at the centre, Gaia as the horizontal ground, Uranus as the height above, and Tartarus as the depth below. These are not gods in the way that Zeus is a god — they are the structural coordinates of existence. The myths that follow, with all their violence and love and betrayal, happen inside the space these primordials define.

The creation account in Greek myth is less a story than a map. It tells you where you are before it tells you what happened there. And what comes next — the reign of Uranus, his overthrow by Cronus, the birth of the Olympians — follows directly from the tensions baked into this first arrangement.

A sky that presses down on an earth. A father who fears his children. A mother who will not forget. These were the conditions before any story began.
