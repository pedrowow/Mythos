Zeus

King of the Olympians, Lord of the Sky, Father of Gods and Men

Olympian Sky Thunder Law & Order Oaths

Zeus enthroned, Roman marble copy of a Greek original, 1st–2nd century CE — Musée du Louvre, Paris

Zeus is the youngest son of Cronus and Rhea, and the only one of their children not swallowed at birth. He spent his childhood hidden in Crete, which is why he alone grew to adulthood while his siblings aged inside their father's stomach — a fact that makes him technically the youngest Olympian but effectively the first to act.

His character in the myths is not simple. He is the guarantor of cosmic order, the god who punishes oath-breakers and protects suppliants, but he is also vain, lustful, and prone to enforcing his authority through punishment rather than persuasion. The Greeks did not require their gods to be moral exemplars — they required them to be powerful and consistent, and Zeus was both.

His primary weapon, the thunderbolt, was forged by the Cyclopes as a gift after Zeus freed them from Tartarus. It became his permanent attribute — the thing he holds in virtually every depiction. The thunderbolt is not merely a weapon; it is a symbol of divine authority, the physical manifestation of the sky's power over the earth.

Zeus presides over all the Olympians but is not all-powerful in any absolute sense. He can be tricked, outmanoeuvred, and even in some traditions bound. He operates within a cosmic order that precedes him — the Fates are not subject to his will. Even the king of the gods must work within structure rather than above it.
