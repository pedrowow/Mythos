Cronus

King of the Titans; Lord of the Harvest; Devourer of his children

Titan Time Harvest Ruler

Francisco Goya, Saturn Devouring His Son, c. 1819–1823 — Museo del Prado, Madrid

Cronus is the youngest of the twelve Titans, son of Gaia and Uranus, and the only one willing to do what none of his siblings would: castrate his own father at his mother's request. That willingness is his defining characteristic. He is not the strongest Titan, nor the wisest — but he is the most ruthless.

He took the sickle Gaia had made, waited for Uranus to descend at night, and acted. His father's prophecy — that Cronus himself would be overthrown by a son — arrived before the blood had dried.

His solution to the prophecy — swallowing each child with Rhea — is where Greek mythology is at its most psychologically acute. He does not kill them. He incorporates them. The threat stays inside him, contained but alive, and he goes on ruling as though nothing is wrong. This is the archetype of every tyrant who refuses to confront succession: the future isn't destroyed, only delayed, and it grows inside the structure that tried to suppress it.

In Roman mythology Cronus becomes Saturn, associated with the harvest, time, and a golden age of plenty. The figure softens considerably — the devouring becomes metaphorical (time consumes all things). The Greeks were less interested in making him sympathetic.
