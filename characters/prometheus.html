Prometheus

Titan; Fire-bringer; Creator of Mankind; Eternal Rebel

Titan Forethought Fire Mankind Punishment

Dirck van Baburen, Prometheus Being Chained by Vulcan, 1623 — Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam

Prometheus's name means "forethought" — and it is this quality, the ability to see consequences before they arrive, that makes him both the most useful ally in Greek mythology and the most dangerous. He sided with Zeus in the Titanomachy not out of loyalty but because he could see which side would win.

In some accounts he shapes mankind from river clay. In all accounts he is their protector, their advocate before the gods, and eventually their benefactor in the act that will define him forever: he steals fire from Olympus — carried in a hollow fennel stalk — and gives it to humans.

The punishment Zeus devised was elaborately cruel. Prometheus was chained to a rock in the Caucasus Mountains, and each day an eagle came to eat his liver. Each night, because he was immortal, the liver grew back. The next day, the eagle returned. This continued until Heracles eventually killed the eagle and freed him.

Prometheus is the closest Greek mythology comes to a figure who suffers for humanity's benefit. But he is also sharper, more calculating, and entirely without martyrdom's comfort. He knew what would happen. He chose it anyway. Whether that makes him heroic or simply stubborn is a question the Greeks left open.
